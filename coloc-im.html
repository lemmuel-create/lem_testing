Concept

When several radios share one product and transmit at the same time, nonlinear parts mix their signals. Those parts include the PAs, front-end switches, and even corroded or ferrous metal. The mixing creates harmonics (n·f) and intermodulation products (a·f1 ± b·f2) at frequencies none of the radios was certified for. Each module's own grant covers only its own emissions, so the host has to show that the combination is clean too (KDB 996369).

A product matters most when it lands in a §15.205 restricted band, where an absolute field-strength limit applies. When an LTE or CBRS transmitter is one of the sources, its −13 dBm mask (Part 96: −25/−40 dBm/MHz) applies wherever the product falls. A product on another radio's receiver in the same product, such as GNSS or LTE downlink, is a performance problem rather than an FCC one. Method notes
  • Ranges are exact for the occupied channels. A product a·f1 + b·f2 spans every combination of frequencies inside each channel, so a 20 MHz Wi-Fi channel widens a 3rd-order product to 40 MHz.
  • Orders computed: harmonics n·f (2nd to 10th); 2nd order f1 ± f2; 3rd order 2f1 ± f2 and 2f2 ± f1; optional 5th order 3f1 − 2f2 and 3f2 − 2f1. Lower orders are usually stronger, so the table sorts by severity, then order.
  • The planner predicts where products can fall, not how strong they are. Levels depend on PA linearity, antenna isolation and duty cycle, and only a radiated measurement settles them.
  • Harmonics go up to the 10th by default, matching the emission search range in §15.33(a) and §2.1057(a). Products from Part 15 radios are cut at 40 GHz; products from LTE/CBRS alone continue to the 10th harmonic.
  • Restricted bands are listed from 30 MHz up (§15.205); everything above 38.6 GHz is restricted. Receive bands are the host radios' own (for example LTE downlink and GNSS L1/L5).
